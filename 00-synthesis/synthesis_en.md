## Thesis

Even the most transparent DAOs we studied spend a great deal of effort deciding how to share value that already exists. They are much less systematic about stating what new value a decision is supposed to create and how that outcome will be measured.

We did not set out to reach this conclusion. We set out to find which DAO actions are associated with a token doing better than the market. We found no robust evidence that buybacks, direct distributions or burns raise the token price by themselves, or that one form works better than another. Of everything we pre-registered, only protocol fee growth was consistently associated with token returns against ETH over a year, and the relationship was far from one-to-one, with both measured over the same period. In 2023–2026 the market also did not price DeFi tokens as a simple claim on current cash flow, though a supplementary 2022–2023 window pointed the other way on very few observations. When we then asked which DAO decisions grow the business, we ran into a measurement problem twice. Even at the most transparent protocols, DAO spending cannot be recovered from treasury outflows by automatic rules, and where actual spending is reported, about three in four major spending and incentive decisions have no goal that can be checked against public data a year later.

## Summary

- Buybacks, direct distributions and burns. No robust evidence that they raise the token price by themselves, and no evidence that a more direct form beats a buyback. The only exception is a positive reaction to the first public announcement of a buyback programme, based on just seven events with a wide spread, and it does not repeat on later changes.
- Business growth. Fee growth over the same year is the only pre-registered factor that survived all pre-specified checks in the main 2023–2026 analysis; in the supplementary 2022–2023 window it had the same sign and similar magnitude, but was fragile. The relationship is far from one-to-one. Ten percent more fee growth went with about 2.5% more token return against ETH. Fee growth and returns were measured over the same period, so this describes what separated winners from losers rather than predicting them.
- Valuation. At equal fee scale, protocols that kept a larger share of fees were not valued higher. Tokens that looked cheap relative to their business did not later outperform expensive ones.
- Spending. Treasury outflows do not track reported spending, even at the most transparent protocols. Neither of two automatic methods passed the pre-registered test, and the gaps are structural.
- Capital allocation. In four of the most transparent DAOs, which are not a random sample, 54 of 58 major spending and incentive decisions stated a goal, but only 14 stated it in terms that public data can check. Among those 14, we found no pattern that was robust across DAOs and reasonable counting choices.
- Governance implication. Every material budget or incentive programme should state, before the vote, one public metric, its baseline, the expected direction or target, the review date and who will publish the review.

The main estimates come from the 2023–2026 regime; the supplementary 2022–2023 window is small. All results describe associations, not proven causes.

## What we tested

All price results are measured against ETH. A holder of a DeFi token could always have held ETH instead, and measuring against ETH removes market-wide swings.

We approached the question in six studies, each covering a weakness of the one before.

1. Event study of buyback decisions, by stage, from the first announcement to launch and later changes.
2. Comparison of forms of value transfer, namely direct distributions, buy-and-distribute, and buybacks into the treasury.
3. Annual token trajectories for more than a hundred protocols, with each token's return against ETH decomposed into business growth, rerating and supply dilution.
4. What the market pays for at the time of valuation, separating growth of the whole category from gains against competitors, and fee scale from the share of fees the protocol keeps.
5. A validation test of whether DAO spending can be measured from on-chain treasury data.
6. Case studies of four DAOs, covering how they allocate resources and what happened to the business after their decisions.

Every deviation from the written method is logged with its reason in all six studies. Studies 3 to 5 were pre-specified and frozen with a checksum before any computation. In study 6, the method, decision tables and goal map were frozen before outcomes were joined, but the cross-DAO pattern rule contained an ambiguity discovered after the results. Every study went through an independent review. Every review found errors, we fixed them, and we withdrew conclusions that did not survive the fixes. As a supplementary robustness check for studies 3 and 4, we added one window, October 2022 to October 2023, the end of the bear market after Terra and FTX. This window is not fully blind. We had seen median token returns during the survivorship check that preceded it, and the window was chosen after that check, although no regressions were run before the method was frozen. We rejected an April 2022 window because Terra-ecosystem and other tokens that lost almost everything against ETH drop out of the data.

## What survived

**Fee growth.** Study 3 covers 202 observations of 110 protocols over three annual periods, October to October, 2023 to 2026. The last period carries 95 of the 202 observations. The backdrop was harsh, with only 18%, 9% and 24% of tokens beating ETH in the three years, while the median token lost 27%, 60% and 42% against ETH. Fee growth had the strongest association with token returns. The top fifth of the sample beat ETH by a median 16% with fee growth of 24%. The bottom fifth lost 80% with fees down 68%. About 30% of the observed variation in returns is associated with fee growth in this specification. When a protocol's fees grew 10% faster than a peer's, its token beat the peer by about 2.5% on average, and the rest was absorbed by a change in the multiple. The supplementary 2022–2023 window gave this finding additional but weak support. The relationship had the same sign and a similar size, a coefficient of 0.21 against 0.26, but with 28 observations its significance was sensitive to the choice of standard errors and to individual tokens.

**The market did not price tokens as a simple claim on current cash flow.** Study 4 decomposed returns further into growth of the protocol's category, change in its share of that category, monetisation and rerating. It tested three pre-registered hypotheses, and none held, but two of them gave informative negative answers. At equal fee scale, protocols that kept a larger share of fees were not valued higher. The coefficient is close to zero, and its interval rules out the market valuing protocols on revenue alone. Across protocols, market capitalisation rose much less than proportionally with fees. A protocol with twice the fees was worth about 30% more on average.

The shortest statement the data support is that in 2023–2026 the market responded to the size of a business and how it changed, but did not value DeFi tokens as a simple claim on current cash flow. This applies to the main 2023–2026 periods. In the supplementary 2022–2023 window the signs were reversed, with a higher take rate associated with a higher valuation and cheap tokens later doing better than expensive ones, but this rests on about 10 informative observations in three categories and carries little weight.

**First announcements of buybacks.** Study 1 covers 43 events in 16 protocols in 2022–2026, including buybacks, direct distributions and supply reductions, each dated from primary sources. Every event was compared with two controls, a fixed basket of large DeFi tokens and tokens in a similar state on the event date without an event of their own. A claim was accepted only when the sign agreed on both controls. Among buybacks, a positive reaction on both controls appears only for the first public announcement of a programme. Over 30 days the token beat the basket by a median 4.2 percentage points and similar tokens by 12.0 points. There are only seven such events, the spread around the median is about 40 points, and the strongest reactions coincided with other news at the same protocols.

## What did not

**Later stages of buybacks.** Governance votes showed no positive reaction. Late activations and changes to an existing mechanism showed a small negative, about 4 points against both controls. Larger programmes did not produce better outcomes, whether measured in dollars, as a share of revenue or as a share of trading volume, though the sample has no large programmes. We saw no premium for burning purchased tokens and no penalty for buybacks funded from the treasury, though there are only three such cases.

**The form of value transfer.** A common view holds that the market values a more direct flow to holders, such as payouts in ETH or stablecoins, above a buyback. Study 2 found seven cases where a protocol introduced one of the forms or switched between them. No form had a consistent advantage. Six of seven came in below the main control, one of them practically at zero, and four of the seven could only be compared with the basket. Both introductions of direct distributions from zero did worse than the control. So did the one switch from buyback to direct distributions, and both switches in the other direction. An early signal in favour of direct distributions disappeared once we checked what holders actually received rather than what the mechanism described. Two of these introductions, at Balancer and Jito, were in effect fee switch activations, but there are only a handful of such cases, and we did not test fee switch activations systematically. Seven cases are too few to prove that forms are equal, but we found nothing in favour of a more direct flow.

**Holder share of fees.** In the main annual periods of study 3, at equal fee growth, tokens that passed a larger share of fees to holders looked better. A pre-registered check on periods shifted by six months did not replicate this, so we treat it as an artefact of one specification. A forward test for October 2026 to October 2027 is registered and its starting data are frozen.

**Market share against category growth.** Study 4 found that growth in market share and growth of the whole category were associated with price equally weakly. One percent more growth corresponded to roughly 0.2% more token return, and no difference between the two was detected, though the test had limited power. The descriptive picture is striking. In most observations, protocols in the sample lost share within their category over the year, with a median loss of 30% to 47%, even though for the typical protocol the category's fees grew faster than ETH in all three periods. The data cannot tell whether that share went to new entrants or to protocols without a token, and part of the loss may come from DefiLlama gradually starting to track new competitors. In the supplementary 2022–2023 window 72% of protocols also lost share, but this is partly mechanical, because DefiLlama nearly tripled the number of tracked products during that year.

**Cheapness.** Tokens that were cheap relative to their business did not later outperform. In the main periods the association is insignificant, and in the shifted periods expensive tokens later did better than cheap ones. A natural explanation, which we did not test, is that the gap between valuation and simple business metrics contains information those metrics miss, such as growth expectations, team quality and future monetisation, rather than market error.

**Other factors.** New product versions, expansion to new chains and token unlocks showed no significant association with returns. Tokens with shrinking supply looked better, but this rests on a few tokens with burns and is not shown for inflationary tokens on their own. Tokens of protocols that suffered a hack looked worse, but hacks were included in the model only as a control. Neither survives correction for multiple testing.

## Why DAO spending is hard to measure

If business growth is what goes with token performance, the next question is which DAO decisions grow the business, and above all which spending. Only five of the sixty largest protocols in the sample publish actual spending for two years or more, and for one of them, Olympus, only budgets exist for the relevant years. The only way to get spending for a large sample is to compute it from treasury outflows. Before drawing any conclusions, study 5 tested whether such outflows match the spending DAOs report. The pass criteria were set in advance. The typical error had to be within about 40%, there could be no systematic bias between protocols, and the year-on-year direction had to match.

Neither of two methods, one based on transfers and one on daily treasury balances, passed. The typical error was more than twofold. Using transfers, the ratio of outflow to reported spending ranged from 0.15 at Sky to 6.4 at Uniswap. The reasons are structural and differ by protocol. Lido's treasury tops up committees regularly, so its outflow is closest to spending, though still about one and a half times higher. Aave's treasury includes a reserve that pays staker rewards. Sky's spending does not pass through the treasury at all, because funds are minted directly to recipients. Uniswap's outflow reflects large tranches to its foundation rather than what the foundation spent. A treasury is not an expense ledger. The right number can only be obtained by labelling each recipient by hand, which is a case study, not a metric for a large sample.

## What DAOs do with their resources

Study 6 therefore studies the four remaining cases with actual reported spending, Lido, Aave, Sky and Uniswap. DAO decisions from 2023–2025 were selected by pre-set materiality thresholds, and the decision tables and the mapping of goals to metrics were frozen before any outcome data were joined. The study covers 112 decisions.

The main result is about whether a decision can be evaluated at all. Of 58 spending and incentive decisions, 54 state a goal, but only 14 state it in terms that are published for all protocols, namely fees, revenue, category share, TVL or token price. The rest describe goals that nobody measures systematically, such as liquidity depth, number of integrations or governance participation. These DAOs explain why they spend, but rarely tie the goal to a public metric, and they are the most transparent DAOs, not a random sample.

Among the few decisions that can be checked, we found no pattern that holds across DAOs. The most common type is user incentives aimed at growth, five decisions in four DAOs. A year later, category share had risen after the Lido and Aave programmes and fallen after the Sky and Uniswap programmes. We checked our rule for a cross-DAO pattern under every reasonable way of counting. It was met in only two variants, and those two point in opposite directions. This does not mean incentives fail. It means existing disclosures cannot show that any type of spending systematically works better than another.

The four DAOs also allocate resources in very different ways. Lido spent about as much as it earned in revenue in 2024–2025. Aave's spending fell from about 0.9 to 0.44 of revenue as revenue grew. Sky spends around half of its revenue. The Uniswap DAO had almost no revenue until the end of 2025 and funded its foundation in tokens. Discussing value accrual without asking how the organisation is built, who receives the revenue and who actually bears the costs makes little sense.

## What this means for governance

Buybacks, distributions and burns should not be sold to a community as a way to raise the token price. We found no reliable evidence of such an effect beyond the reaction to a first buyback announcement, which does not repeat on later changes. A larger share of fees kept by the protocol brought no valuation premium, and the link between holder share and returns did not replicate on shifted periods. Fee switch activations are rare in our samples and were not tested systematically. Choices about the form and size of distributions are worth debating on other grounds, such as tax and legal consequences, transparency, alignment and treasury discipline.

Among the factors we tested, the state and growth of the protocol's business were the most consistently associated with token returns against ETH. When assessing a token, it therefore makes sense to look at the business measured in the unit in which the holder compares alternatives. Revenue that rises in dollars only because ETH rises adds nothing for a token holder compared with holding ETH. Market share deserves separate attention, because categories grew while most protocols in the sample lost share within them.

Our data do not show which DAO decisions grow the business. Partly this is because the sample of decisions we could evaluate is very small and even the sign of the result depends on how it is measured. Partly it is because DAOs rarely create the data such a check needs. This leads to the most practical recommendation of the series. Every material budget or incentive programme should state, before the vote, one primary public metric, its baseline value, the expected direction or target, the review date, and who will publish the review. Without this, a year later nobody can tell a good expense from a bad one, and every budget debate starts from scratch.

## Lido as a case study

This section applies the results to one protocol. It is not itself a research result.

Lido shows why the unit of measurement and market share matter. In dollars, the protocol's annual revenue rose from $31M in October 2023 to $50M in October 2025 and fell back to $32M by October 2026. In ETH it fell from 18.6k to 12.1k ETH over the first two years and stood at 11.8k ETH in October 2026. Lido's share of fees in ETH liquid staking, with ETH staking separated from staking on other chains as in study 4, was 89% in October 2023, 84% in 2024, 64% in 2025 and 70% in 2026. Measured against all liquid staking protocols tracked by DefiLlama, as in study 3, the share is lower, 88% in 2023 and 53% in 2026, but the direction is the same. LDO underperformed ETH in all three years, by 50%, 46% and 34%, which is not unusual among staking tokens.

The last year is the most telling. Lido regained some market share, and the share of fees it keeps rose after the DAO cut the base node operator fee in the Curated Module. But the whole ETH staking market shrank by about 30% against ETH, Lido's own multiple fell, and the ratio of LDO market cap to annual revenue dropped from about 43 to about 12 over three years. Better operating metrics did not turn into a better token. This is consistent with the study 4 finding that the market paid no premium for monetisation, though that finding compares different protocols rather than changes within one.

A further observation concerns NEST, the mechanism that directs part of the surplus to buying LDO into the treasury. It has operated since August 2026. According to the contract on 6 October 2026, its budget was negative, about −$541k, and no purchases had been made. Study 6 shows that Lido's spending was 1.07 and 1.19 times DAO revenue in 2024 and 2025, or 0.88 and 1.04 excluding token compensation under the Token Reward Plan. Revenue here is DefiLlama's, the DAO treasury's share of protocol fees after the node operator share. If the purchase threshold is tied to spending and spending roughly equals revenue, the size of purchases becomes a direct function of the DAO's budget policy. This is an implication for Lido, not a research result. Our results also give no reason to expect that replacing NEST with direct distributions would improve the LDO price by itself. But the samples contain almost no switches to treasury buybacks, and there are not enough data on LDO purchases for a conclusion either way, so these studies do not evaluate NEST's parameters and are not an argument for or against its current design.

## Limitations

Samples are small in all six studies, especially in study 2 on forms and study 6 on resource allocation. Most of the data come from 2024–2026, a single market regime, and the supplementary 2022–2023 window has only 28 observations, with a survivorship check that gives only a lower bound on missing tokens. Fee and revenue data come from DefiLlama and depend on how DefiLlama computes them, and category share is sensitive to when DefiLlama started tracking competitors. Categories follow the current classification, since we have no history of categories. CoinGecko circulating supply is sometimes revised retroactively, so we also measured dilution on-chain. DAO spending reports are prepared by different firms using different methods. Many tests were run, and individual significant results may be due to chance, so we rely only on what holds across all checks and different approaches.
